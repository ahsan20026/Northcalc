How to Get Cheap Car Insurance in Canada (2026 Guide)

Canadian car insurance is among the most expensive in the world. In BC, the average driver pays over $1,800/year. In Ontario, $1,900+. But with the right moves, most drivers can cut 20–40% off their premium.

Why Canadian Car Insurance Is So Expensive

Canada's auto insurance landscape is unlike almost any other country. Depending on which province you live in, you might be buying coverage from a government monopoly, a private insurer, or a hybrid of both. That complexity — combined with some other uniquely Canadian factors — helps explain why premiums here rank among the highest in the developed world.

In Ontario, the root of the problem is a generous accident benefits system combined with high litigation costs. Insurers pay out not only for property damage but for medical rehabilitation, income replacement, and attendant care — and the legal system allows plaintiffs to sue for pain and suffering on top of that. The result is that insurers price those potential costs into every policy.

Across all provinces, rising vehicle repair costs are a major driver of premium increases. Modern vehicles are loaded with sensors, cameras, and driver-assistance technology — even a minor fender-bender can require $3,000–$8,000 in repairs to recalibrate systems and replace specialty parts. Electric vehicle repairs are even more expensive, with battery damage alone costing tens of thousands of dollars.

Urban density matters too. Drivers in Toronto, Vancouver, and Montreal face dramatically higher premiums than rural counterparts in the same province. More cars on the road means more collisions, more theft, and more claims — all of which get pooled across the insurance market.

Finally, climate-related claims have increased sharply. Hailstorms in Alberta, flooding in BC, and ice storms in Ontario are generating massive comprehensive claims that push up premiums even for drivers in lower-risk areas.

The bottom line on why premiums are high: High litigation costs (especially Ontario), expensive modern vehicle repairs, growing EV claims, urban traffic density, and increasing climate-driven weather events all combine to push Canadian auto insurance premiums upward year after year.

Mandatory vs. Optional Coverage: What You're Actually Paying For

Before you can lower your premium intelligently, you need to understand what you're buying. Canadian auto insurance is split into mandatory and optional coverages, and the distinction matters when deciding what to trim.

Mandatory Coverage (Required Everywhere in Canada)

  • Third-party liability: Pays for injury or property damage you cause to others. Minimum is $200,000 in most provinces, but experts strongly recommend $1 million or more. The cost difference between $200K and $2M in liability is often only $50–$150/year — one of the best value-per-dollar upgrades you can make.
  • Accident benefits: Covers your own medical costs, rehabilitation, income replacement, and attendant care regardless of who caused the accident. Scope varies significantly by province.
  • Uninsured/underinsured motorist: Protects you if you're hit by a driver with no insurance or insufficient coverage — a surprisingly common situation.
  • Direct compensation property damage (DCPD): Available in most provinces; lets you claim against your own insurer for damage caused by another driver, speeding up the process.

Optional Coverage (Often Worth Having, Sometimes Not)

  • Collision: Covers damage to your own vehicle from an accident, regardless of fault. Generally worth carrying if your car is valued above $10,000.
  • Comprehensive: Covers theft, fire, weather damage, vandalism, and animal strikes. Lower cost than collision but covers a wide range of non-accident scenarios.
  • Specified perils: A narrower (cheaper) version of comprehensive that only covers explicitly listed risks.
  • Loss of use / rental car coverage: Pays for a rental while your car is being repaired. Useful if you don't have a second vehicle.
  • Waiver of depreciation: On new vehicles, ensures your insurer pays the replacement cost rather than the depreciated value after a total loss. Typically worthwhile for the first two to three years.
Quick coverage rule of thumb: If your car is worth less than $5,000, dropping collision and comprehensive and banking those premiums yourself often makes more financial sense than paying for coverage that would barely exceed your deductible in a payout.

12 Proven Ways to Lower Your Car Insurance Premium

These strategies are actionable right now — many can be applied at your next renewal. Combined, a typical Ontario or BC driver can realistically save $500–$800/year.

1. Shop and Compare at Every Renewal

Loyalty does not pay in Canadian auto insurance. Insurers price renewals assuming many customers won't bother shopping around, which means long-term customers often end up paying 20–30% more than new customers with identical profiles. Rates can vary by 30–50% between insurers for exactly the same driver, vehicle, and coverage.

At every renewal, get quotes from at least three to five insurers. Use an independent broker (who can quote multiple companies like Intact, Aviva, and Wawanesa simultaneously) or online comparison platforms. The process takes under 30 minutes and can save hundreds of dollars.

2. Bundle Home and Auto Insurance

Most major Canadian insurers offer multi-policy discounts when you insure both your home and vehicle with the same company. The typical saving is 10–15% on both policies combined. Desjardins, Intact, Aviva, and TD Insurance are among the carriers with strong bundling discounts. If you're a renter, a tenant's insurance + auto bundle often qualifies as well.

3. Increase Your Deductible

Your deductible is the amount you pay out-of-pocket before insurance kicks in. Moving from a $500 to a $1,000 deductible on collision and comprehensive coverage typically saves 10–20% on those components. The key question: do you have $1,000 in savings available if you need to make a claim? If yes, the higher deductible is almost always the right financial choice. If not, build that cushion first.

4. Install Winter Tires

Quebec mandates winter tires. Ontario and New Brunswick strongly incentivize them through insurance discounts. Across most provinces, declaring winter tire use to your insurer earns approximately a 5% discount on your premium — typically $75–$120/year. Winter tires also meaningfully reduce your risk of at-fault accidents in winter conditions, which protects your claims record. The discount effectively pays for part of your tires over a few seasons.

5. Use a Telematics App

Telematics programs — where an app or device monitors your acceleration, braking, cornering, speed, and driving times — are offered by most major Canadian insurers. Intact MyDrive, Aviva Drive, Wawanesa Savings, and Desjardins Ajusto are among the most widely used programs. Safe drivers typically save 10–30% on their premiums. The catch: if your driving habits score poorly, some programs may adjust your rate upward (though most allow you to opt out before any increase applies). If you're a smooth, low-mileage driver, telematics is one of the highest-return options available.

6. Take an Approved Driving Course

For new and young drivers, this one is particularly impactful. Completing a recognized defensive driving course — such as Young Drivers of Canada or a provincial driving school's certified program — can earn a 10–20% discount with many insurers. The discount reflects statistically lower crash rates among trained drivers. Even experienced drivers can sometimes earn a smaller discount (5–10%) by completing a refresher course.

7. Keep a Clean Driving Record

This is the most powerful long-term lever you control. A single at-fault claim typically increases your premium by 15–25% for three to six years. A minor conviction like a speeding ticket adds 10–25%; a distracted driving conviction in Ontario or BC can add 25–40%. The financial logic of "should I make this claim?" deserves careful thought — if your claim is only slightly above your deductible, paying out-of-pocket and protecting your record may cost less in the long run than filing.

8. Reduce Your Declared Annual Mileage

Drivers who cover fewer kilometres make fewer claims, and insurers price this in. Honestly declare your actual annual kilometres — if you've switched to remote work, started taking transit more, or moved closer to work, update your insurer. Under 10,000 km/year earns meaningful discounts with most insurers. Some carriers now offer pay-per-kilometre models: Intact My Pace and Onlia are among those operating in Ontario. For truly low-mileage drivers (under 8,000 km/year), pay-per-km can cut insurance costs substantially.

9. Pay Annually Instead of Monthly

Monthly payment plans are convenient but not free. Insurers typically charge 3–5% in implicit financing fees for spreading payments across 12 months. Paying your annual premium in full saves that charge and eliminates the risk of NSF fees or lapses from a missed payment. If cash flow is a concern, set up a separate savings account and contribute monthly — then pay the annual premium from it at renewal.

10. Review Coverage on Older Vehicles

Collision and comprehensive coverage make financial sense when your vehicle is worth significantly more than the cost of those coverages plus your deductible. As cars age and depreciate, the math shifts. If your vehicle's market value has dropped below $3,000–$5,000, the maximum you'd ever receive from a total-loss claim may be less than what you'd pay in premiums over the next few years. Dropping collision and comprehensive on an older paid-off vehicle can save $300–$600/year.

11. Improve Your Credit Score (Where Permitted)

In Alberta, Ontario, and the Atlantic provinces, insurers are permitted to use credit-based insurance scoring as a rating factor. Research consistently shows a correlation between credit management and insurance claim patterns. A higher credit score typically translates to a lower-risk classification and a lower premium. Paying bills on time, reducing credit card balances, and avoiding unnecessary credit inquiries all contribute to score improvement — and to insurance savings in provinces that allow it.

12. Ask About Every Available Group Discount

Many Canadians leave money on the table simply because they don't know to ask. Alumni associations, employer benefit plans, professional groups (CPA Canada, nursing associations, engineering associations, law societies), and clubs like CAA often have negotiated group rates with specific insurers. When you contact an insurer or broker, explicitly ask: "What group or association discounts do you offer?" The answer may surprise you.

Insurance by Vehicle Type: What Costs More to Insure

Your choice of vehicle has a direct and significant impact on your insurance premium. Insurers set rates by vehicle based on claims history: how often that model is involved in accidents, how expensive it is to repair, and how frequently it's stolen.

  • Luxury and performance vehicles: Highest premiums. Expensive to repair, expensive to replace, and frequently targeted for theft. A BMW 3 Series or Audi A4 can cost $1,500–$2,500 more annually to insure than a comparable mainstream sedan.
  • Electric vehicles (EVs): Increasingly expensive to insure as of 2025–2026. Battery damage — even from minor collisions — can cost $15,000–$30,000+ to repair or replace. A shortage of certified EV repair shops adds cost and delay. Many insurers have raised EV rates significantly.
  • Full-size SUVs and trucks: Higher premiums than sedans, primarily due to elevated repair costs for body panels, sensors, and cameras. Pickup trucks are also high theft targets in many provinces.
  • Honda Civic, Toyota Corolla, Mazda3: Consistently among the cheapest vehicles to insure in Canada. Parts are affordable and readily available, repair times are short, and these models have good safety records. If minimizing insurance cost is a priority, mainstream Japanese sedans and hatchbacks are hard to beat.
  • The red car myth: Vehicle colour has absolutely no effect on insurance rates. Insurers don't know what colour your car is — they rate by make, model, year, and trim level, not paint.

High-Risk Drivers: Your Options in Canada

Not every driver qualifies for standard market rates, and understanding your options matters if you're in a high-risk category.

New and Young Drivers (G1/G2 in Ontario)

New drivers in Ontario — particularly those under 25 — face some of the highest premiums in North America. G1 and G2 drivers can pay $3,000–$8,000/year for standalone policies. The most effective strategies are to be added as an occasional driver on a parent's policy (a major saving), choose a low-risk vehicle, select a high deductible, and complete a recognized driving course for the associated discount. Telematics programs are especially valuable for new drivers who have clean habits — they can override the youth penalty with demonstrated safe driving.

Drivers with DUI or Serious Convictions

A DUI conviction in Canada typically makes standard market insurance unavailable for three years post-conviction. Affected drivers are placed through the Facility Association — a shared pool of high-risk drivers that all licensed insurers are required to participate in. Premiums through Facility Association run two to three times the standard market rate. After the conviction period ends and you've maintained a clean record, standard rates gradually become accessible again. Some specialty insurers focus on high-risk customers and may offer more competitive rates than the Facility Association minimum.

High-risk tip: Named driver exclusions can be useful for families where one member has a poor driving record. Excluding a high-risk driver from the policy keeps premiums manageable — but that driver cannot legally operate the vehicle under any circumstances.

Public vs. Private Insurance: How It Works by Province

Understanding whether you're dealing with a government insurer or a private market is essential context for shopping and comparing.

BC — ICBC

Basic Autoplan coverage is mandatory and purchased exclusively through ICBC, BC's provincial insurer. Optional enhanced coverage can be purchased through ICBC or through private brokers offering ICBC-backed extensions. As of 2024, ICBC shifted to a driver-based rating model — your driving record and experience determine your rate, not the vehicle you drive. This was a significant change that benefited high-record drivers and penalized those with convictions or claims more directly than before.

Manitoba — MPI

Manitoba Public Insurance provides basic coverage under a no-fault system. Injury claims are handled entirely by MPI — you cannot sue another driver for pain and suffering, which keeps costs and premiums lower than Ontario. Optional extensions can be purchased through private brokers. Manitoba's no-fault model is often cited as a model for controlling insurance costs without sacrificing coverage quality.

Quebec — The Split Model

Quebec's hybrid system explains its uniquely low premiums. The SAAQ (Société de l'assurance automobile du Québec) handles all bodily injury claims on a no-fault basis — funded through driver's license and registration fees. Private insurers compete only for property damage coverage. With bodily injury claims entirely removed from the private market, competition for property damage cover is fierce and premiums are low. Quebec drivers pay roughly half of what Ontario drivers pay for equivalent coverage.

Ontario — Fully Private, Most Complex

Ontario has the largest and most competitive private auto insurance market in Canada, but also the most expensive. The province has a no-fault accident benefits system (meaning you claim medical/rehabilitation benefits from your own insurer) but retains a tort system that allows lawsuits for pain and suffering above a threshold. This combination — generous benefits plus litigation rights — is the primary reason Ontario premiums lead the country. Drivers here benefit most from aggressive comparison shopping and bundling.

The Cheapest Insurers in Canada: 2026 Overview

The "cheapest" insurer varies meaningfully by driver profile, vehicle, location, and coverage level — there is no single answer. That said, these carriers are consistently competitive for specific segments:

  • Wawanesa: Consistently competitive in BC, Manitoba, and Saskatchewan. Strong claims service reputation and solid telematics discounts.
  • Belairdirect: Direct-to-consumer model with lower overhead than broker-distributed insurers. Competitive for clean-record drivers in Ontario and Quebec.
  • Economical Insurance: Strong pricing for drivers with excellent records. Available through brokers across Canada.
  • Desjardins: Dominant in Quebec, strong in Ontario. Excellent multi-policy discounts. Ajusto telematics program is among the most generous in Canada for safe drivers.
  • CAA Insurance: Member discounts make this competitive for Ontario drivers who are or become CAA members. Strong roadside assistance integration.
  • Sonnet Insurance: Digital-first insurer targeting low-risk drivers in Ontario, Alberta, and Atlantic Canada. Fast quoting, competitive for clean-record customers.
  • Pembridge Insurance: Competitive in Alberta and Ontario, particularly for rural and suburban drivers.

The key takeaway: never assume your current insurer is the cheapest option. The market changes year to year, and your own risk profile changes too — a year without claims, a birthday that moves you into a lower age bracket, or a new driving course can open up better pricing you won't access unless you actively shop.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does my credit score affect car insurance in Canada?

It depends on your province. In Alberta, Ontario, and the Atlantic provinces, insurers are permitted to use credit-based insurance scores as a rating factor. The logic is that credit management patterns correlate statistically with insurance claims patterns. In BC, Manitoba, Saskatchewan, and Quebec, credit scoring for insurance purposes is either prohibited or heavily restricted. If you're in a province where it applies, improving your credit score by paying bills consistently and reducing revolving debt can translate directly into lower premiums over time.

Is it worth making a small insurance claim?

Often not. A single at-fault claim typically increases your premium by 15–25% and that surcharge can last three to six years. On a $1,800/year policy, a 20% surcharge lasting four years costs you an additional $1,440 in cumulative premiums — before you consider losing your claims-free discount. If the damage is only slightly above your deductible ($200–$500 above it), paying out-of-pocket almost always costs less than claiming. The claim threshold worth filing changes depending on your premium, your insurer's forgiveness policy, and how many previous claims you've had.

Do electric vehicles cost more to insure in Canada?

Yes, increasingly so. While EVs were often insured at near-standard rates when they first entered the market, that has changed significantly in 2025–2026. Battery repairs — even from minor collisions — are enormously expensive. A partial battery replacement on a popular EV can cost $20,000–$40,000. The shortage of certified EV repair facilities extends claim duration and rental car costs. Most major Canadian insurers have raised EV premiums, and some have tightened underwriting. If you're purchasing an EV, get insurance quotes before you finalize the purchase decision.

Can I insure a car that's not in my name?

Generally, you can insure a vehicle you don't own, but it's more complex than insuring your own car. Most insurers want to see an "insurable interest" — a legitimate financial stake in the vehicle. Common situations where this works: a spouse insuring a vehicle registered in the other partner's name, a parent insuring a vehicle registered to an adult child living in the same household, or a business owner insuring a company vehicle. Where it gets complicated is insuring a vehicle registered to someone in a different household. Always disclose the ownership situation to your insurer — misrepresentation on an insurance application can result in claims being denied.

What is the minimum car insurance required in Ontario?

Ontario requires four coverages at minimum: third-party liability (minimum $200,000, though $1 million+ is strongly recommended), statutory accident benefits, direct compensation property damage (DCPD), and uninsured automobile coverage. Collision and comprehensive are optional. Nearly all Ontario drivers would benefit from carrying at least $1 million in liability — the cost difference is small and the protection difference is enormous if you're ever in a serious accident.
