How to Use This Calculator

  1. Enter the amount you want to withdraw. Try the figure you have in mind, then test a slightly smaller one — crossing a withholding threshold can cost far more than the extra dollar is worth.
  2. Select your province. Quebec plans have a lower federal withholding rate because Revenu Québec withholds provincial tax separately.
  3. Enter your marginal tax rate. This is the rate on your last dollar of income, not your average rate. It is usually the highest bracket you touch — check your most recent notice of assessment if you are unsure.
  4. Enter your other taxable income for the year. The withdrawal stacks on top of this, which is why it is often taxed at a higher rate than you expect.
  5. Read the cash figure first. This is what actually lands in your account after withholding.
  6. Compare withholding against estimated actual tax. The difference line is the important one — if it says you owe, that is a bill arriving at filing time, not now.
  7. Plan for the gap. If the difference is significant, either set it aside deliberately or withdraw in a year when your other income is lower.

Withholding Is Not the Tax You Owe

This is the most important thing to understand about RRSP withdrawals. The amount your institution withholds is a prepayment, set at a flat rate by the CRA. Your actual tax is determined when you file, based on your total income for the year and your marginal rate.

Those two numbers often disagree. If you withdraw $20,000 on top of a $60,000 salary, the withholding is 30% ($6,000), but that $20,000 sits in your top bracket, which could be over 43% depending on your province. The gap arrives as a tax bill the following April.

Planning note: withholding rates ignore your province and your income level entirely. Before making a large withdrawal, work out the real marginal cost — or set aside the difference deliberately. Withdrawing in a year when your other income is low is one of the few genuinely effective ways to reduce the tax on RRSP money.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the withholding rate applied in tiers like income tax?

No. Withholding is applied to the entire withdrawal at a single rate. Withdrawing $15,000 costs 20% ($3,000), but withdrawing $15,001 costs 30% on the whole amount ($4,500.30) — nearly $1,500 more for one extra dollar. If you are near a threshold, withdrawing slightly less can leave you meaningfully better off in the short term.

Will I get the withheld tax back?

Only if the withholding turns out to be more than your actual tax. Because RRSP withdrawals are taxed at your marginal rate and withholding is a flat, bracket-based rate, low-income years and small withdrawals can produce a refund, while large withdrawals on top of a decent salary usually produce a bill.

Does an RRSP withdrawal affect my OAS or other benefits?

Yes. Unlike a TFSA, an RRSP withdrawal counts as taxable income. It raises your net income for the year, which can trigger the OAS recovery tax, reduce the Guaranteed Income Supplement, and affect income-tested benefits such as the Canada Child Benefit. A large one-off withdrawal can have knock-on effects well beyond the tax on the withdrawal itself.

Should I withdraw from my RRSP or my TFSA?

In general, spend TFSA money first if you want to keep your taxable income low, because TFSA withdrawals do not affect benefit clawbacks. RRSP withdrawals are more attractive in a year when your other income is unusually low. The right answer depends on your bracket now versus your expected bracket later.
